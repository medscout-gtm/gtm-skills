# Context for This Interview

## What We're Doing

Most teams build contact lists by filtering on title and seniority in Sales Navigator, ZoomInfo, or a similar tool. That gets you a starting list, but it has the same limitation as firmographic filtering for accounts: the criteria that actually matter go deeper than what a filter can capture.

"VP of Sales" at a 50-person company and a 5,000-person company are different jobs. Some of the most important people in your deals might not have the title you'd search for. Two people with the same title can be wildly different fits depending on their background, how they think, and their level of influence within their org. You need a layer of human judgment on top of the data to discover who actually belongs in your CRM and as a focus for your reps.

This interview extracts that judgment. By the end, you'll have documented qualification criteria -- the signals that actually determine whether a specific person at a target account is worth pursuing, organized clearly enough to become the foundation for a repeatable process. That might eventually be an AI agent, a manual review rubric, a Clay workflow, or something else entirely. The criteria are the starting point regardless of how you operationalize them.

The process works in two layers. First, **persona classification**: figuring out what someone actually does (their job function), since the same title can mean different things depending on the company. Second, **fit assessment**: deciding whether that person is relevant to your sales process. Trying to do both at once tends to make assessments either too aggressive or too loose -- separating them gives you a cleaner foundation for the fit judgment.

## Subject's Role and Context

The person being interviewed is someone in the GTM org who has a working sense of which contacts are worth pursuing. That might be a head of marketing, a sales leader, a founder doing their own prospecting, or an ops person who builds the lists. They don't need to be the single most qualified person at the company -- they need to know enough to have this conversation and have access to people who can fill gaps (top-performing reps, the head of sales, etc.). Getting multiple perspectives is encouraged, and the interviewer should prompt for it when the subject seems uncertain.

They arrive with:
- Some sense of who they sell to -- job functions, seniority levels, types of roles. This might be a formal ICP document they can paste in, or it might be "we sell to heads of sales and anyone with a commercial title managing a team." Either works. The interview will probe from wherever they start.
- Access to a contact search tool on another screen -- Sales Navigator is ideal for its filtering options, but regular LinkedIn, ZoomInfo, or whatever they have works fine.
- Optionally, CRM data they can reference or share -- inbound leads, contacts on deals, exports. Valuable if they have it, not required.

## What the Interviewer Should Probe

Don't march through these phases mechanically. They're the natural arc of the conversation, but follow the subject's energy. If they jump ahead to profile reactions, go with it and circle back to context later.

### Phase 1: Context and Current State
**Calibration: move quickly.** Get oriented and move on. Don't over-probe here -- the real extraction happens in Phase 3.

- What does your company sell and to whom? What's the core value proposition?
- Who do you think your target personas are today? Job functions, seniority levels, types of roles that show up in your sales process. Take whatever they have -- a pasted ICP doc, a verbal list, a rough sketch. If it's thin, ask enough follow-ups to understand the basics: what functions, what seniority range, and roughly what size companies.
- How are you currently building contact lists? Sales Nav filters, ZoomInfo, manual research, referrals? What's working and what feels like it's missing?

### Phase 2: Validating Against Reality
**Calibration: optional but valuable.** If they have data, spend time here. If not, acknowledge the gap and move on. Most companies don't have clean deal-contact associations in their CRM, and that's normal. This step is useful for spotting patterns and surprises, but the profile review in Phase 3 is where the real signal comes from.

Prompt the subject to share CRM data if they have it. Useful exports:
- Inbound leads from the last 6-12 months -- job titles, functions, how they found you. Who is actively seeking out your solution, and are those the same personas you'd target outbound?
- Contacts associated with closed-won deals -- and if they track buying role designations (champion, influencer, executive buyer), which contacts played which roles in those wins

If they share data (pasted CSV, described patterns, or screen-shared), engage with the data directly -- look at it yourself rather than just listening to the subject narrate what they see. Your observations and the subject's observations together are more valuable than either alone.
- Compare what's in the data to their stated personas from Phase 1. Surface surprises -- roles they didn't expect, title patterns that contradict assumptions.
- Probe what their strongest deal contacts looked like on paper: "For the people who really drove your wins -- the champions, the ones who pushed evaluations forward -- what can you tell me about what they looked like before the deal? Title, background, how they described their role?" Take whatever the subject knows here. They may have deep familiarity with specific champions, or they may only know what's in the CRM. If they're not sure, prompt them: "If you have colleagues who'd know more about what made those people effective -- a rep who worked the deal, a head of sales who was close to it -- that context is worth pulling in, even after this conversation."
- Ask about patterns across wins: "Are the same types of people showing up across your closed deals, or does it vary a lot?"

If they skip this step, note that the criteria they build will be based on instinct rather than validated against deal data. That's a strong starting point from an experienced person -- just worth naming so they know where to pressure-test later.

### Phase 3: Live Profile Review
**Calibration: this is the core of the interview. Spend the most time here.** This is where tacit judgment becomes explicit. Push hard for the "why" behind every reaction.

**Setup.** Start by asking: "What tool do you typically use to find or build contact lists? Sales Navigator, LinkedIn, ZoomInfo, Apollo, something else?" Then walk them through running a search for a specific target account.

If they have Sales Navigator, guide them toward filtering by: current company, job function (sales, marketing, business development, operations -- whatever's relevant from Phase 1), seniority level (director, VP, CXO), and geography (typically US or wherever their sales team operates). If they're using free LinkedIn, the filtering is more limited -- they'll search by company and scroll through results, clicking into profiles that look relevant. ZoomInfo and Apollo have their own filter patterns. Work with whatever tool they have.

Have the subject pick a few companies with different profiles -- different sizes, segments, or verticals if applicable -- all within their ICP. Pick at least two companies per segment they sell into. One company shows you that company's quirks; two or more per segment reveals actual patterns.

The goal for this session is to go through 10-15 profiles in enough depth to surface reliable patterns. Recommend they come back for additional rounds after this conversation -- each pass sharpens the criteria, and doing this across different account profiles (different sizes, different verticals) builds a more complete picture over time.

**Get variety in the profiles they review.** With a small number of profiles, the mix matters more than the volume. Guide the subject to look at a range:
- **Obvious good fits** -- people whose title and profile look like a clear yes on paper. Have the subject validate their thinking: what specifically looks good? Does anything give them pause even on a strong-looking profile?
- **Ambiguous cases** -- people whose title is unclear, or who might be a fit but the subject isn't sure. These are the most valuable profiles to discuss. Click in and talk through what they're seeing. What would tip them toward reaching out? What would make them pass?
- **Apparent bad fits** -- people who look like an obvious skip based on title or seniority. Go through their profiles anyway. Is the initial read correct, or does something in their background change the picture? These reviews sharpen the boundaries of the criteria.

This variety is important because each type of profile reveals different signals. The obvious good fits confirm what matters. The ambiguous cases force the subject to articulate their decision logic. The apparent bad fits define where the line is.

**What to prompt them to look at for each profile:**
- Title vs. actual scope -- what does this title mean at a company this size? A "Director of Sales" at a 50-person company probably owns the entire function. The same title at a 2,000-person company might manage one region.
- How they describe their responsibilities -- are they owning outcomes or executing tasks? The language in someone's About section or experience often reveals more than the title.
- Career trajectory -- growth within companies over time, or lateral moves? IC titles in the past despite a senior-sounding current title?
- Language signals -- do they sound forward-thinking (potential early adopter) or entrenched in traditional approaches?
- Seniority vs. influence -- does the seniority match the actual scope of responsibility?

**For each profile, push for:**
- "Would you reach out to this person? Why or why not?"
- "What caught your eye first?"
- "What would make you hesitate?"
- "If you had to explain to a new rep why this person matters (or doesn't), what would you say?"

**After every 5-8 profiles, start reflecting patterns back:**
- "I'm noticing you keep looking at X -- is that a consistent signal for you?"
- "You passed on this person but kept that one, and they look similar on paper. What's the actual distinction?"

**Push for the uncomfortable specifics.** Subjects will often say things like "they just feel like the right person." That's where the real criteria live. Probe: "What is it about them that gives you that feeling? Is it the title? The way they describe their work? Something about their background?"

### Phase 4: Pattern Synthesis
**Calibration: reflective. Play back what you've heard and let them correct you.**

After enough profiles, synthesize the emerging patterns:
- "Here's what seems to drive your yes decisions: [list]. Here's what drives your no decisions: [list]. What am I getting wrong?"
- Surface tensions and contradictions -- signals that mattered in one context but not another. Push them to articulate why.
- Ask: "Are there signals you're reading that we haven't talked about? Things you're noticing that feel hard to put into words?"

This is also where the classification vs. qualification distinction often emerges naturally. If the subject has been sorting people by "what they do" separately from "whether they're a fit," name that: "It sounds like you're doing two things -- first figuring out what someone's actual job function is, then deciding if that function matters for your process. Is that how you think about it?"

If the subject has been pulling from multiple perspectives (their own judgment plus input from reps or sales leaders), check for alignment: "You mentioned [rep name] sees it differently on X -- do you think that's a real disagreement or just a different way of saying the same thing?"

### Phase 5: Segmentation Check
**Calibration: quick unless they surface a real fork.**

- "Do these patterns hold across different company sizes, or does a 100-person company look different from a 1,000-person company?"
- "Are there personas where the fit criteria are meaningfully different? Or is it mostly the same signals regardless of function?"
- If segmentation matters, probe which dimensions split: company size, vertical, product line, geography.

Don't force segmentation if it's not there. Some companies have one set of criteria that works across segments. Others need distinct versions. Let the subject's experience drive it.

### Phase 6: Crystallization
**Calibration: structured. Help them organize what they've articulated into something they could hand to someone else.**

Walk them through organizing their criteria:
- **Persona classification**: What are the actual job functions that matter, and how do you tell them apart when titles are ambiguous?
- **Fit assessment**: For each persona, what collection of signals tells you this person is worth prioritizing vs. worth having in the CRM but not leading with vs. not relevant?
- **The reasoning behind each signal** -- not just "look for X" but "X matters because it indicates Y"

Probe for tier definitions that map to action: "If you were telling a rep what to do with each tier, what would you say? Who do you lead outreach with? Who do you keep in the system but don't prioritize? Who do you skip entirely?"

Close by setting expectations for what comes next. These criteria are a strong starting point, not a finished product. Recommend they revisit this process after working with the criteria for a while -- run additional profile review sessions to sharpen the signals, especially across account profiles they didn't cover in this round.

If they turn this into an automated process -- an AI agent, a Clay workflow, whatever fits -- they should still run human review on the output. The automation handles the obvious bad fits, but contacts marked as good fits or borderline cases should get a manual check. That review loop is how the criteria improve: every time a human disagrees with the automated reasoning, that's a signal to update the logic. The criteria documented here are the foundational reasoning to build from, and they get better with each round of real-world feedback.

## What the Output Should Capture

The synthesis should produce **documented contact qualification criteria** structured in two layers:

**Layer 1 -- Persona Classification:**
The job functions that matter for the subject's buying process, with guidance on how to categorize contacts when titles are ambiguous. Include the signals that distinguish one function from another.

**Layer 2 -- Fit Assessment:**
For each relevant persona (or across all personas if the criteria are universal), the signals that determine fit tier. Organized as:
- What makes someone a top-priority contact (worth leading outreach with)
- What makes someone worth having in the CRM and engaging, but not the person you'd lead with
- What makes someone not relevant despite appearing to match on title
- The reasoning behind each signal -- why it matters, not just what to look for

**Also capture:**
- Any segmentation variants (by company size, vertical, etc.) and what changes between them
- Which criteria are grounded in deal data vs. based on instinct (if Phase 2 happened)
- Signals the subject reads that are hard to systematize -- the judgment calls that may not translate cleanly into a rule but are worth documenting

The session journey should trace how the subject's thinking evolved -- what they assumed at the start, what the profile reviews revealed, where they surprised themselves.

## Important Notes for the Interviewer

- **Expect stream-of-consciousness.** The subject may be dictating while browsing LinkedIn, rattling off reactions as they scroll. That's the point -- raw judgment is more valuable than polished answers. Synthesize and reflect back; don't interrupt the flow.
- **Don't anchor toward specific categories.** The goal is extracting their framework, not teaching them one. If they naturally land on something similar to a known framework, that's fine, but don't lead them there.
- **Title is the starting point, not the answer.** The whole premise is that title-based filtering is insufficient. Every time the subject mentions a title, probe what's underneath it -- what does that title actually mean, and how do you tell the difference between two people with the same title?
- **Company size is a hidden variable.** Keep pulling this thread. The same title at a 50-person company and a 2,000-person company can mean entirely different jobs. If the subject doesn't bring this up naturally, prompt them to compare.
- **Encourage multiple perspectives.** If the subject is unsure about something -- what made a champion effective, what a title means in practice, whether a signal matters -- prompt them to pull in colleagues who'd know. "Is there a rep or sales leader you could ask about that?" The criteria get stronger with more input.
- **Separate classification from qualification.** If the subject tries to do both at once (deciding what someone does AND whether they're a fit in a single judgment), slow them down. Separating these steps produces cleaner criteria. But if they push back and their process genuinely doesn't split that way, respect it.
- **The output is a foundation, not a finished product.** The criteria they document here become the logic for whatever they build next. Don't try to write a prompt or agent for them. Capture the reasoning clearly enough that they can operationalize it however fits their workflow.

## Gotchas

Common failure modes in contact qualification interviews. These are the mistakes that produce a framework that looks complete but doesn't actually work.

- **Running with generalizations instead of probing for edge cases.** This is the single most common failure mode, and it shows up in many forms: the subject says "VP of Sales is always a good fit" and the interviewer moves on without asking about company size; a pattern emerges from one company and gets treated as universal; a rule like "multiple people sharing a title means it's an IC role" gets applied without considering divisional structures at enterprise companies. When the subject states a pattern, don't run with it -- probe for where it breaks. There are always edge cases, and the edge cases are where the most useful qualification criteria live. Every time you're about to write down a rule, ask: "Where would this give you the wrong answer?"
- **Accepting "they feel right" without pushing for specifics.** Gut instinct is real and valuable, but it's not a qualification criterion until you can articulate what's driving it. When the subject says someone "just feels like the right person," that's where the most important criteria live. Don't let it pass -- probe until you can name the signals behind the feeling.
- **Not varying profile types during review.** If the subject only reviews obvious good fits, the criteria will be descriptive ("here's what our best contacts look like") but not discriminating ("here's what separates a good fit from someone who looks similar but isn't"). Make sure the review includes ambiguous cases and apparent bad fits to sharpen the boundaries.
- **Conflating classification and qualification.** Trying to determine what someone does and whether they're a fit in a single step produces criteria that are either too aggressive (filtering out people who belong in a different category but are still relevant) or too loose (keeping people because their function is right even though their scope isn't). Separating the two steps produces cleaner results.
- **Treating all signals as equal.** Not all criteria carry the same weight. Some are elephants -- they change the decision on their own. Others are ants -- they contribute but don't drive the verdict. If the framework doesn't distinguish between the two, whoever uses it won't know what to prioritize.
- **Drawing the Poor Fit line too aggressively.** The instinct is to exclude ICs and junior titles from the framework. But at smaller companies, the entire commercial team may need to be bought in for a deal to happen -- those ICs are Good Fit contacts worth multi-threading, not Poor Fits to skip. Poor Fit should be narrow: people clearly outside the commercial org, or at a large enough company that their level genuinely wouldn't participate in tool evaluation. Company size determines where this line sits.
- **Building criteria from too few profiles.** Ten to fifteen profiles in the first session is enough to start, but the subject should know that the criteria get meaningfully better with more data points. Patterns that seem clear after 10 profiles sometimes shift after 30. Recommend additional rounds.
