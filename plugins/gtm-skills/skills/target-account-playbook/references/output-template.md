# Output Template: Qualification Framework

This is a reference file for the qualification framework generator. It defines the structural pattern for the output document — what sections to include, what goes in each, and how the pieces relate. The generator builds this document section by section with the user, not all at once.

This template is a default structure, not a rigid format. If the user's company needs something different — a third layer, a different organizing principle, a different per-criterion anatomy — adapt. The structure should serve the company's reality, not the template.

---

## Document Structure

### 1. Context and framing

This section sets up the rubric. It's the difference between handing someone a table and handing them a table with the thinking that makes it usable.

**What belongs here:**
- What the conversations revealed — the themes, patterns, and surprises that emerged across stages. Synthesize, don't recap. The reader shouldn't need to know there were stages; they should understand the thinking.
- How the company thinks about qualification — the distinction between "could we sell to them?" and "should we?" in this company's terms. What makes their qualification problem specific.
- How the criteria connect — which ones tend to go together, which ones compensate for each other, where the framework gives clear answers vs. where it requires judgment.
- How to use this document — specific to this company. Where the criteria in each layer come from (research vs. sales conversations), what tools or sources are realistic, how rigid vs. flexible the framework is meant to be.

**Voice:** This should read like the leader wrote it. Use their language, their examples, their way of describing the problem. If the leader said "we need companies that are actually doing outbound, not just thinking about it," that's better than "the prospect should demonstrate active outbound sales activity."

**Length:** A few paragraphs to a page. Long enough to make the rubric interpretable, short enough that people read it.

### 2. Layer 1: Pre-engagement criteria

These are criteria the team can assess before any outreach or sales conversation. They answer: "Should we pursue this account?"

**What belongs here:** Criteria that are assessable from outside-in research — company websites, LinkedIn, job postings, news, financial data, public information. A rep should be able to evaluate these without talking to anyone at the company.

**Per criterion:**

- **What you're looking for** — the attribute or trait, described concretely. Not "strong commercial presence" in the abstract, but what that means for this company in terms someone could look up or observe.
- **Why it matters** — the connection between this criterion and customer success or deal viability. Ground this in what came up during the conversations — real customer examples, the rep's experience, the leader's reasoning. Not generic logic.
- **What good looks like** — specific, observable indicators that this criterion is met. Concrete enough that two different reps would assess it the same way. If the criterion has levels (strong / acceptable / weak), describe what each looks like.
- **What would be concerning** — the flip side. What indicates this criterion isn't met, or is borderline. Include the gray areas — not just clear disqualifiers, but the situations where it's a judgment call.
- **How rigid this is** — is this a hard boundary (if not met, don't pursue) or a judgment call (weigh it, document your rationale, proceed with eyes open)? Be explicit. If it's a hard boundary, say so plainly. If it's a judgment call, describe the range — what would push it toward "proceed anyway" vs. "walk away."

**Ordering:** Lead with the most decisive criteria — the ones that filter out the most accounts or that are easiest to assess. If a company fails criterion #1, there's no point evaluating criteria #2-5.

### 3. Layer 2: Pre-close criteria

These are criteria that can only be assessed during the sales process — through conversations, demos, discovery calls, and the buying process itself. They answer: "Is this deal going to work?"

**What belongs here:** Criteria that require interaction with the prospect to evaluate. Champion strength, use case fit, buying process complexity, stakeholder alignment — things that aren't visible from outside research.

**Per criterion:** Same anatomy as Layer 1 (what to look for, why it matters, what good looks like, what's concerning, rigidity). But the signals are different — they come from conversations, not research.

**Relationship to Layer 1:** Layer 2 builds on Layer 1, not replaces it. An account that passed Layer 1 still needs to pass Layer 2. But Layer 2 criteria may also reveal that a Layer 1 assessment was wrong — a company that looked strong from outside might have issues that only surface during the sales process. Note where that's a known risk.

### 4. Open questions

Things the framework doesn't resolve. This section is honest about what's still uncertain.

**What belongs here:**
- Patterns that emerged but aren't solid enough to be criteria yet — "we saw this in two companies, but we're not sure if it's a real signal or coincidence"
- Criteria the leader was uncertain about — where they said "I think this matters but I'm not sure"
- Gaps the rep's process revealed but didn't explain — things the rep does that work but neither the leader nor the rep could articulate why
- Areas where the customer examples contradicted each other — where good-fit and bad-fit companies looked similar on a given dimension
- Suggested next steps for testing — how the company might validate or refine these open questions over time

**Why this section matters:** A framework that claims certainty everywhere is less useful than one that's honest about what's known and what's still being figured out. This section gives the team permission to keep learning and makes the framework feel like a living document rather than a finished product.

## Formatting Notes

**Use the user's language consistently.** If the leader calls their sales team "AEs," use "AEs." If they say "commercial team," say "commercial team." Don't standardize to generic B2B terminology.

**Be concrete over abstract.** "Companies with 50+ US-based sales reps" is better than "companies with a mature commercial organization." Where the conversations produced specific thresholds, numbers, or examples, use them.

**Tables work for some criteria, prose works for others.** Layer 1 criteria with clear levels (yes / maybe / no) may work well as a table. Layer 2 criteria that require more nuanced judgment may be better as structured prose. Use whatever format makes each criterion most usable for the people who'll actually reference the framework day-to-day.

**The document should be self-contained.** Someone reading it shouldn't need to know how it was built. No references to stages, cartridges, or the process. It should read like a qualification framework, not like the output of a tool.
