# Signal Idea Bank

This is a reference file for the qualification framework generator. It organizes signals by what they help you understand about a prospective account — why the signal matters, not where to find it or when to check it.

Use this to broaden the user's thinking during framework generation. When a user identifies a criterion that matters (e.g., "we need companies with a mature sales team"), this bank helps you suggest signals they might not have thought of. It's a brainstorming tool, not a checklist. Not every category will be relevant to every company.

---

## Company fit

**What you're trying to understand:** Is this the kind of company where your product works? Not just "could they use it" but "will they succeed with it."

**Why it matters:** Most products work well for a specific shape of company and poorly outside that shape. The criteria are different for every vendor — size, industry, business model, stage — but nearly every B2B company has boundaries, even if they haven't articulated them yet.

**Signals to consider:**
- Revenue range or employee count — not as a vanity metric, but because your product may require a certain operational scale to be useful
- Industry or vertical — some products are horizontal, but even horizontal products tend to win in certain industries and struggle in others
- Business model — B2B vs. B2C, subscription vs. transactional, product-led vs. sales-led. The way a company makes money shapes what they need.
- Geographic footprint — where they operate, where their customers are, whether they're domestic or international. Relevant when your product has geographic constraints or when geography affects the problem you solve.
- Company structure — divisions, subsidiaries, franchise vs. corporate. Affects who buys, how decisions get made, and whether one deal means one deployment or many.

## Growth and trajectory

**What you're trying to understand:** Where is this company headed? A company's direction often matters more than its current state.

**Why it matters:** A company that's growing into your sweet spot is a better prospect than one that's currently there but contracting. Trajectory affects urgency, budget, willingness to invest in new tools, and whether the problem you solve is getting more or less acute.

**Signals to consider:**
- Hiring patterns — not just headcount growth, but what roles they're hiring for. A company adding its first VP of Sales is in a different moment than one replacing their third.
- Funding or investment events — new rounds, debt financing, PE acquisition. Each changes the company's priorities and spending capacity in different ways.
- Market expansion — new geographies, new verticals, new product lines. Expansion often creates the exact growing pains your product solves.
- Revenue trajectory — when available, whether the company is growing, flat, or declining. Public companies disclose this; private companies signal it through hiring, office expansion, or press.
- Strategic shifts — pivots, rebrandings, new leadership. These moments create both opportunity (new priorities) and risk (everything is in flux).

## Problem awareness and urgency

**What you're trying to understand:** Does this company know they have the problem you solve? And is something making it urgent right now?

**Why it matters:** A company with a severe problem they don't recognize is a harder sell than one with a moderate problem they're actively trying to fix. Awareness and urgency determine whether you're educating or solving — and how long the sales cycle will be.

**Signals to consider:**
- Active search behavior — are they looking for solutions? Job postings for roles that address the problem, RFPs, vendor evaluations, event attendance.
- Trigger events — leadership changes, regulatory shifts, competitive pressure, a bad quarter, a lost customer. Something that makes the status quo untenable.
- Public pain — complaints on review sites, analyst coverage of their challenges, press about struggles in the area you address.
- Workaround indicators — they're solving the problem with spreadsheets, manual processes, or a tool that wasn't built for it. They know they have the problem; they just haven't invested in a real solution.
- Inbound behavior — if they're engaging with your content, attending your events, or visiting your site, that tells you something about awareness. But absence of inbound doesn't mean absence of need.

## Organizational readiness

**What you're trying to understand:** Even if this company needs your product, are they set up to actually adopt it?

**Why it matters:** A company can have the right problem and the right budget but still fail as a customer if they don't have the team, process, or maturity to implement and use what you sell. This is the category most qualification frameworks underweight — it's harder to assess from the outside, but it's where a lot of bad-fit deals originate.

**Signals to consider:**
- Team existence and maturity — do they have a team that would own your product? How established is it? A company that's still building the function you sell into will adopt differently than one with a mature team.
- Process maturity — do they have established processes in the area your product supports, or are they still figuring it out? Some products require existing process to plug into; others help create process from scratch.
- Technology stack — what other tools do they use? Complementary tools suggest maturity in the area. Absence of related tools might mean they're not ready, or it might mean there's a greenfield opportunity. Context matters.
- Previous investments in the category — have they tried to solve this before? If so, what happened? A company that churned from a competitor has different readiness than one that's never tried.
- Internal advocacy — is there someone inside who understands the problem and is pushing for a solution? This is hard to assess from outside but often surfaces during sales conversations.

## Buying capability

**What you're trying to understand:** Can this company actually make the purchase? Not just "do they have money" but "can they get a deal done?"

**Why it matters:** Some companies have the need, the budget, and the intent, but their procurement process is so complex that deals die of attrition. Others can move fast when they want to. Understanding buying capability helps you predict deal velocity and avoid spending months on a deal that was never going to close.

**Signals to consider:**
- Budget indicators — not just company revenue, but whether they allocate budget to the category you sell in. A large company with no budget line for your category is harder than a smaller company that already spends in your space.
- Decision-making structure — centralized vs. distributed purchasing. A single decision-maker vs. a buying committee. Each requires a different sales approach and has different cycle time implications.
- Procurement complexity — do they have a formal procurement process? Vendor security reviews? Legal review cycles? These aren't disqualifying, but they affect timeline and effort.
- Past purchasing behavior — how have they bought similar tools before? If they're a customer of products adjacent to yours, they've already navigated whatever internal buying process exists.
- Authority clarity — can you identify who would approve this purchase? If the authority is clear and accessible, deals move faster.

## Competitive landscape

**What you're trying to understand:** What is this company using today to address the problem you solve, and how entrenched is it?

**Why it matters:** Replacing an existing solution is a different motion than filling a gap. The competitive landscape tells you about the company's awareness of the problem, their expectations for a solution, switching costs, and what you'd need to prove to win.

**Signals to consider:**
- Current solution — do they use a competitor? A homegrown tool? Nothing? Each implies different sales dynamics.
- Entrenchment level — how deeply integrated is their current solution? A tool that touches one team is easier to replace than one embedded in company-wide workflows.
- Contract timing — are they mid-contract, approaching renewal, or month-to-month? Timing affects when they can realistically switch.
- Satisfaction indicators — reviews, public feedback, job postings mentioning migration. Dissatisfaction with a current tool is a strong buying signal.
- Competitive gaps — where does their current solution fall short? If you know your competitors' weaknesses, you can assess whether this company is likely to feel them.

## Stakeholder dynamics

**What you're trying to understand:** Who inside this company would care about what you sell, and what's the relationship between them?

**Why it matters:** Products get bought by people, not companies. The presence or absence of a champion, the alignment (or conflict) between stakeholders, and the political dynamics around the problem you solve all affect whether a deal happens and how it goes.

**Signals to consider:**
- Champion existence — is there someone inside who would advocate for your product? This is often the single strongest predictor of deal success, and the hardest to assess from outside.
- Stakeholder roles — who would use the product, who would buy it, who would approve it? The fewer handoffs between those roles, the simpler the deal.
- Organizational alignment — is there tension between teams about how to solve the problem? Misalignment can stall deals or create political risk.
- Leadership stability — recent leadership changes in the relevant function can be opportunity (new leader wants new tools) or risk (everything is on hold until they settle in).
- Relationship history — have they talked to your company before? Positive or negative past interactions set the stage for the current conversation.

## Use case fit

**What you're trying to understand:** Will your product actually work for what this company needs to do with it?

**Why it matters:** A company can check every other box — right size, right industry, right budget, strong champion — and still be a bad customer if the way they'd use your product doesn't match what it's built for. Use case fit is often the gap between "qualified on paper" and "successful in practice."

**Signals to consider:**
- Primary use case — what would they use your product for? Is that a use case where your product excels, where it's adequate, or where it's a stretch?
- Scale requirements — does their volume, complexity, or scope match what your product handles well? A product built for mid-market might struggle at enterprise scale, or vice versa.
- Integration requirements — does your product need to connect to their existing systems? Are those integrations supported, feasible, or blockers?
- Customization needs — will they need the product to work out of the box, or will they need significant configuration? How much customization can your team realistically support?
- Success definition — what would "working" look like for them? If their definition of success doesn't match what your product delivers, misalignment is inevitable.

## Risk indicators

**What you're trying to understand:** What might go wrong with this account — even if everything else looks good?

**Why it matters:** Qualification isn't just about finding reasons to pursue an account. It's also about spotting reasons a deal or customer relationship might fail. Some risk indicators are disqualifying; others are just things to manage. The framework should help the team distinguish between the two.

**Signals to consider:**
- Financial instability — layoffs, declining revenue, credit risk. A company in financial trouble might buy but fail to implement, renew, or pay.
- Organizational chaos — frequent leadership turnover, recent mergers or acquisitions, public culture issues. These create implementation risk even when the buying signal is strong.
- Unrealistic expectations — when a company's stated goals for your product don't match what it actually does. This surfaces during sales conversations more than research.
- Bad-fit history — if similar companies have churned, struggled with implementation, or required disproportionate support, that pattern is relevant.
- Single-threaded deals — when the entire deal depends on one person's enthusiasm. If they leave or lose influence, the deal or the customer relationship collapses.

## Using This Bank

**During framework generation:** When the user identifies a criterion, use this bank to ask better follow-up questions — not to suggest a list of signals. If the user says "we care about whether they have a mature sales team," you might ask: "What does that actually look like for you — is it about headcount, specific roles existing, how long the team has been in place?" You're drawing from the bank to probe, not to present options. The user should feel like you're asking good questions, not reading from a reference sheet.

**Don't present the categories.** This is an organizing structure for Claude, not for the user. Never surface these as categories, lists, or menus. If the user says "we need to know if they can actually buy," you might draw from the buying capability section to ask sharper questions — but you'd never say "let's go through the buying capability signals" or "here are five things to consider."

**Every company's version is different.** A signal that's critical for one company is irrelevant for another. The user's cartridge conversations — especially the fit analysis and the rep's process — tell you which of these categories carry the most weight. Lead with what came up in those conversations, then broaden from there.

**Signals are hypotheses until tested.** A signal from this bank that the user hasn't validated against real customer examples is a suggestion, not a recommendation. Frame it that way: "This might be worth looking at" not "you should track this."
