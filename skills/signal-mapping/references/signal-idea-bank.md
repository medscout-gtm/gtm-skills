# Signal Idea Bank

This is a reference file for the signal mapping skill. It organizes signals by source -- where the data comes from -- and tags each with what it can tell you: account fit, person fit, timing, or engagement approach. Some signals map to multiple categories.

Use this to broaden the user's thinking during signal discovery. When the user describes their tools and channels, or when you're working through what they're trying to learn, draw from this bank to float specific ideas they might not have considered. It's a brainstorming tool, not a checklist. Not every signal will be relevant to every company.

---

## First-party signals

Actions someone takes on your own channels. These are unique to you -- competitors can't see them. Most teams undercount what they have here.

**Website behavior**
- Pricing or demo page visits -- strong timing signal. Someone actively evaluating. Also an engagement signal: the page they visited tells you what angle to lead with. [timing, engagement]
- Specific product or solution page visits -- tells you which problem resonates. Can indicate account fit if the page maps to a specific use case or segment. [account fit, engagement]
- Blog or resource engagement -- weaker on its own but useful in combination. Repeat visits to content around a specific topic suggest problem awareness. [timing, engagement]
- Return visits after going quiet -- a timing signal. Someone who visited months ago and comes back is re-entering an evaluation cycle. [timing]

**Content and event engagement**
- Content downloads (whitepapers, guides, templates) -- the topic tells you what problem they're thinking about. Gated content gives you contact information. [engagement, person fit]
- Webinar or event attendance -- timing signal (they're investing time in the topic) and person fit signal (the person who registers is often the one with the problem). [timing, person fit]
- Ad engagement -- which ad creative and which audience segment they came from tells you what resonated. Useful for shaping the approach. [engagement]

**Email and outreach responses**
- Email opens and clicks -- weak individually, stronger in patterns. Someone who opens every email but never clicks is different from someone who clicks through to a specific resource. [timing, engagement]
- Sales outreach responses -- the strongest first-party timing signal. Someone who replies, even to decline, has engaged. The content of their reply is high-signal for engagement approach. [timing, engagement]
- Unsubscribes or opt-outs -- a negative signal worth tracking. Not a "do not contact" necessarily, but a signal that the current approach isn't landing. [engagement]

**Product engagement (if applicable)**
- Feature usage in a freemium or trial -- which features they use tells you about use case fit. Heavy usage of a specific feature set suggests they're solving a specific problem. [account fit, engagement]
- Usage frequency and depth -- a timing signal for conversion. Active users are warmer. Users who plateau or drop off may need a different approach. [timing]
- Seats or users added -- a timing and account fit signal. Growth within the product suggests organizational adoption. [timing, account fit]

## Second-party signals

Data from partners, platforms, or collaborative relationships. Many companies have fewer of these, and that's fine. Surface what exists.

**Partner and co-marketing**
- Co-marketing lead lists -- partners who share leads from joint campaigns. The partner's audience often overlaps with yours in useful ways. Person fit signal (who engaged) and timing signal (they engaged recently). [person fit, timing]
- Partner referrals -- a strong account fit and timing signal. Someone a partner sends your way has been pre-qualified by someone who knows the space. [account fit, timing]
- Joint event attendees -- similar to your own events but with the partner's audience mixed in. Useful for identifying accounts outside your current reach. [person fit, timing]

**Platform and ecosystem**
- Tech platform engagement data -- some platforms surface how their users engage with adjacent tools or categories. If you're in an ecosystem (Salesforce AppExchange, HubSpot marketplace, etc.), platform-level signals about who's browsing or evaluating in your category can be valuable. [timing, account fit]
- Integration usage -- if your product integrates with other tools, which integrations a prospect uses tells you about their stack and workflow. [account fit, engagement]
- Review site activity -- G2, TrustRadius, and similar platforms sometimes share data about who's researching your category. Category-level research is a timing signal. [timing]

**Distribution and channel**
- Channel partner intelligence -- distributors, resellers, or VARs who have relationships with your target accounts often have context you don't: who's evaluating, what's driving the decision, what the timeline looks like. [timing, account fit]
- Industry group or association data -- membership lists, committee participation, conference attendance from industry bodies. Useful for account fit and person fit in specialized markets. [account fit, person fit]

## Third-party signals

Things happening in the market or at specific companies that you can observe from the outside. These are the most commoditized -- everyone has access to the same data. The edge is in knowing which ones actually matter for your business and how you respond differently than everyone else.

**Leadership and organizational changes**
- New hire in a role you care about -- person fit signal (the right person just arrived) and timing signal (new leaders make vendor decisions early in their tenure). Because everyone acts on this, your approach needs to stand out. [person fit, timing]
- Leadership departure -- timing signal. The departure of a champion or blocker changes the dynamic. Also a risk signal if you're already in a deal. [timing]
- Reorganization or team restructuring -- can signal shifting priorities, new budget allocation, or a window where established vendor relationships are being reconsidered. [timing, account fit]

**Financial and investment events**
- Funding rounds -- timing signal. New capital often means new vendor evaluations and willingness to invest. The stage and size of the round shape what it means: a Series A company has different needs than a growth-stage company raising a Series D. [timing, account fit]
- M&A activity -- can go either way. Acquiring company may be consolidating tools. Acquired company may be frozen. But M&A often creates a window where everything is up for review. [timing]
- Earnings or financial results -- for public companies, earnings calls surface strategic priorities, areas of investment, and areas of concern. What the CFO emphasizes is a signal about where budget is going. [timing, account fit]

**Hiring patterns**
- Hiring volume in the department you sell into -- a growth signal. If they're adding headcount in the function your product supports, that suggests investment and potentially growing pains. [timing, account fit]
- Specific role postings -- the job description is high-signal. If they're hiring for a role that would own or use your product, that's both timing (they're building the team) and person fit (the person they hire may be your contact). [timing, person fit]
- Hiring for roles adjacent to your product -- sometimes the signal isn't the direct buyer but the team around them. A company hiring data engineers may not buy your analytics tool directly, but it signals investment in the data function. [account fit, timing]

**Competitive and market signals**
- Competitive displacement -- a company dropping a competitor is one of the strongest timing signals. They've already decided to change; the question is what they're moving to. [timing]
- Contract renewal timing -- if you know when a competitor's contract is up, the months leading up to renewal are a natural evaluation window. [timing]
- Review site activity or sentiment -- negative reviews of a competitor's product, especially around problems your product solves, are both timing and engagement signals. [timing, engagement]
- Technology stack changes -- adopting a tool that's complementary to yours suggests the account is moving in your direction. Dropping a tool that integrates with yours is a risk signal. [account fit, timing]

**Regulatory and industry shifts**
- New regulations affecting your target market -- can create urgency across an entire segment. Companies that fall under new requirements often need new tools to comply. [timing, account fit]
- Industry events or trends -- macro shifts (AI adoption, privacy regulations, market consolidation) create contexts where your product becomes more or less relevant. Broad signal, but useful for prioritizing segments. [account fit]
- Analyst or press coverage -- a company being highlighted by an analyst or in trade press for a challenge your product addresses is a timing and engagement signal. [timing, engagement]

## Using This Bank

**During signal discovery:** When the user describes their tools, channels, or the signals they're already tracking, draw from this bank to float specific ideas. If the user says "we have website analytics," you might say "some teams find that pricing page visits and return visits after a quiet period are their strongest timing signals from the website -- have you seen patterns like that?" You're drawing from the bank to prompt, not reading from a list.

**Don't present the source labels or categories.** This organizing structure is for you, not the user. Never say "let's go through the first-party signals" or "here are the third-party options." Weave specific ideas into the conversation naturally based on what the user has shared.

**Float a few ideas, then follow up.** Don't dump five signals at once. Surface two or three, let the user react, then go deeper on what resonates or move to the next area. The conversation should feel like brainstorming with someone who has good ideas, not like reviewing a reference sheet.

**Every company's version is different.** A signal that's an elephant for one company is noise for another. The user's context from Phase 1 -- what they sell, who they sell to, their sales motion -- tells you which signals to lead with. Don't treat every signal as equally worth discussing.

**First-party is the edge.** Most teams spend more time discussing third-party signals because they're more visible and more marketed. But first-party signals are the ones competitors can't replicate. If the user breezes past their first-party data, circle back: "You mentioned you have website analytics but we haven't talked much about what you're seeing there -- is that because you're already tracking it well, or because you haven't explored what it could tell you?"

**Signals are hypotheses until tested.** A signal from this bank that the user hasn't validated against real outcomes is a suggestion, not a recommendation. Frame it that way: "This might be worth looking at" not "you should track this."

**Note the category tags but don't recite them.** The tags (account fit, person fit, timing, engagement) help you connect a signal to what the user is trying to learn. If the user says "I need to know when to reach out," you know to draw from signals tagged [timing]. But you'd surface the specific signal, not the tag.

**Always ask where the data comes from.** When a user confirms they track a signal, ask how they actually get that data. Don't assume sources based on the signal type. "You mentioned tracking new hires in commercial leadership -- where does that come from for you?" The answer shapes the signal map: a signal from a tool running automated enrichments is different from one a rep notices manually on LinkedIn. The source affects reliability, speed, and how the signal gets operationalized.
