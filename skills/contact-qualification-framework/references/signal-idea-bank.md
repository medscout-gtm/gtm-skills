# Signal Idea Bank: Contact Qualification

This is a reference file for the contact qualification skill. It organizes signals by what they help you understand about a person at a target account — why the signal matters, not where to find it or when to check it.

Use this to broaden the user's thinking during the profile review and pattern synthesis phases. When a user identifies a criterion that matters (e.g., "I care about whether they actually own the function or just have a senior title"), this bank helps you suggest signals they might not have thought of. It's a brainstorming tool, not a checklist. Not every category will be relevant to every company.

---

## What this person actually does

**What you're trying to understand:** Their real job function — not what the title says, but what they spend their days on and what they own.

**Why it matters:** Persona classification is the foundation of contact qualification. If you get the function wrong, the fit assessment is meaningless. The same title can map to completely different jobs depending on the company, and many of the most important people in a deal hold titles you wouldn't search for.

**Signals to consider:**
- How they describe their responsibilities in their LinkedIn About section or experience entries — "leading a team of 12 reps across three regions" tells you something different than "supporting the sales organization with enablement materials"
- The verbs they use — owning, building, driving vs. supporting, assisting, coordinating. The first set suggests functional ownership. The second suggests they're part of someone else's operation.
- Whether their title maps cleanly to a single function or straddles multiple — "VP of Growth" could sit in sales, marketing, ops, or something that bridges all three. The profile context usually clarifies, but not always. 
- Job function labels in Sales Navigator or similar tools — useful as a starting point but often wrong for non-standard titles. A "Director of Commercial Excellence" might get categorized under operations when they're really a strategic commercial leader.

## Scope and ownership

**What you're trying to understand:** How much of the function this person actually controls — whether they own outcomes or execute work within someone else's scope.

**Why it matters:** Two people with the same title and function can have wildly different levels of ownership. A "Director of Sales" who owns the full revenue number, hires the team, and sets strategy is a fundamentally different contact than one who manages a regional pod within a larger structure. Scope determines whether this person can influence or drive a purchasing decision, and what kind of role they'd play in your sales process.

**Signals to consider:**
- Team size and structure — whether they mention managing people, teams, or regions. "I lead a team of..." is direct. Absence of any team references in a senior title is a signal worth noting.
- Geographic or segment scope — national vs. regional vs. territory-level. "US commercial operations" is different from "Southeast region."
- Budget or P&L language — any mention of owning a number, managing a budget, or being accountable for revenue. This is rare on LinkedIn profiles but extremely telling when present.
- Reporting structure signals — who they reference as peers or leaders. If they describe working "with the VP of Sales" rather than "for," that suggests peer-level scope.
- How many functions they touch — someone who describes working across sales, marketing, and ops likely has broader scope than someone focused on one domain. At smaller companies, this breadth is common and expected. At larger companies, it may signal a strategic or cross-functional role.

## Seniority and influence

**What you're trying to understand:** Three things that are related but not the same — title, seniority, and influence. Title is the label. Seniority is the person's actual experience level and position in the hierarchy. Influence is their ability to move things internally. These often correlate, but they don't have to, and the relationship between them shifts depending on the company's size, structure, and culture.

**Why it matters:** Every company structures titles differently. Some companies use title inflation — "VP" at a 30-person company may have the scope of a Sales Manager at a 500-person company. Some give senior titles early as a retention tool. Others are conservative with titles, meaning a "Director" there carries more weight than a "VP" somewhere else. Understanding where someone actually sits — their real seniority and their real influence — is what matters for qualification. Influence determines whether this person could champion, block, or shape a deal.

**Signals to consider:**
- Career progression within the current company — someone who's been promoted multiple times within the same organization has demonstrated that the company values them. Coupled with the right job function, that often correlates with real seniority and influence, regardless of what the current title is.
- Whether they're referenced by others at the company — if other employees from the same company mention them, tag them, or describe working with them, that's a visibility signal that speaks to influence specifically.
- External visibility — speaking at industry events, publishing, being quoted in press. This suggests the company views them as a representative, which usually tracks with internal influence.
- Title relative to company norms — "VP" means different things at different companies. Rather than reading a title in isolation, compare it to the company's overall title structure if you can see it (from other employees' profiles, the company page, etc.). A "Director" at a company where the highest commercial title is SVP is different from a "Director" at a company where directors report directly to the CEO.
- Tenure and institutional knowledge — someone who's been at a company for 8 years in progressively senior roles has relationships, context, and credibility that a recent hire with a bigger title may not. Time in the organization is often an influence signal independent of title.

## Career trajectory

**What you're trying to understand:** Where this person has been and where they seem to be going — what their career path reveals about their capabilities, mindset, and likely priorities.

**Why it matters:** Trajectory tells you things the current snapshot doesn't. Someone who rose from IC to VP within one company has deep institutional knowledge and demonstrated growth. Someone who's held the same level across three companies in five years has a different profile. Neither is inherently better or worse for your purposes, but the pattern matters for predicting how they'll engage with your product and process.

**Signals to consider:**
- IC to leadership transition — when and how it happened. Someone who made the jump recently is in a different headspace than someone who's been in leadership for a decade. Someone who never made the jump despite a senior-sounding title is a different signal entirely.
- Industry depth vs. breadth — have they spent their career in one industry, or have they moved across sectors? Deep industry experience means they understand the domain. Cross-industry experience may mean they bring outside perspectives (and may be more open to new approaches).
- Company size trajectory — moving from large to small companies (or vice versa) signals something about what they're looking for and how they work. Someone who left a Fortune 500 for a startup is making a deliberate choice.
- Tenure patterns — staying at companies for 5+ years vs. moving every 18 months. Long tenure suggests depth and institutional knowledge. Short tenure might suggest a pattern, or it might reflect the industry norm.

## Mindset and orientation

**What you're trying to understand:** Whether this person is likely to be open to new approaches, tools, and ways of working — or whether they're entrenched in how things have always been done.

**Why it matters:** Two people with identical titles, functions, and scope can respond to your outreach completely differently based on how they think. Someone who's actively exploring new approaches is a different prospect than someone who's executing a playbook they've run for a decade. This doesn't mean one is better than the other as a contact — but it affects how to engage them, what messaging will resonate, and what role they might play in an evaluation.

**Signals to consider:**
- Language about change and innovation — do they describe "transforming" or "building" things? The way people frame their own work reveals how they think about their role.
- Positioning language parallels — whether there's overlap between how this person describes their role or priorities and how your company positions its product. If someone naturally uses language that mirrors your positioning, that's an alignment signal worth noticing. It suggests they're already thinking about the problem you solve in terms that would resonate with your messaging.
- Content engagement — what they share, comment on, or write about on LinkedIn. Someone who engages with thought leadership about new approaches is signaling interest. Someone whose feed is all congratulations posts and company announcements is harder to read.
- How they describe achievements — results-oriented language ("grew revenue 40%", "built a team from 3 to 15") vs. responsibility-oriented language ("responsible for managing the sales team"). The first suggests someone who thinks in terms of impact. The second describes a job.
- References to specific tools, methodologies, or frameworks — signals that they're actively thinking about how to do their job better, not just doing it.
- Education and development — recent certifications, courses, or learning signals. Not a strong signal on its own, but in combination with other evidence, it suggests someone who invests in staying current.

## Company size context

**What you're trying to understand:** How the company's size and structure change what this person's role actually means — and who actually makes purchasing decisions at companies of this size.

**Why it matters:** This is probably the single most common source of misqualification, and it affects two things at once. First, role interpretation: a "Director of Sales" at a 50-person company is likely the most senior sales person in the organization — they own the number, set the strategy, and might be the one who signs a contract. The same title at a 2,000-person company might manage 8 reps in one region. Second, buying dynamics: who makes purchasing decisions, how many people are involved, and how the decision gets made all shift with company size. Your qualification criteria may need to flex based on size, and even if they don't, you need to interpret every other signal through this lens.

**Signals to consider:**
- Role consolidation — at smaller companies, one person often covers multiple functions. A "Head of Revenue" might own sales, marketing, and CS. At larger companies, each of those is a separate leader with a separate team. This affects both how you classify someone and whether they're a decision-maker.
- Organizational depth — how many layers exist between this person and the CEO (or the person who'd approve a purchase). At a small company, the answer might be zero or one. At a large company, it might be four or five. This affects access, authority, and deal velocity.
- Team size relative to company size — "manages a team of 5" means different things at a 30-person company (they manage a huge chunk of the org) vs. a 3,000-person company (they manage a small pod).
- Buying dynamics at this size — who actually makes purchasing decisions at companies of this size? At very small companies, the founder or CEO is often aware of and involved in every vendor decision. At mid-size companies, a functional leader (VP Sales, Head of Marketing) may own the budget and the decision. At larger companies, buying committees form, multiple stakeholders weigh in, and the person who drives evaluation may be different from the person who signs the contract. The interview should surface how the user's buying process changes across the company sizes they sell into.
- Regional/area titles at smaller companies — at companies below a certain size, there's no meaningful distinction between "regional" and "national" in the org. An "Area VP" at a 60-person company isn't managing one region of many — they may be one of two or three senior leaders in the whole commercial org. The threshold where regional titles start meaning genuinely regional scope varies by industry and company structure.
- Whether company size aligns with your product — if your product works best at a certain scale, the contact's experience at that scale matters. Someone whose entire career has been at enterprise companies may not understand (or be interested in solving) the problems your product addresses for mid-market.

## Using This Bank

**During the profile review:** When the subject is clicking through profiles and reacting, use this bank to ask better follow-up questions — not to suggest a list of signals. If the subject says "this person looks too junior," you might ask: "What specifically makes you say that — is it the title, the career path, or something about how they describe their work?" You're drawing from the bank to probe, not to present options. The user should feel like you're asking good questions, not reading from a reference sheet.

**During pattern synthesis:** When you're reflecting patterns back to the subject, the bank helps you name what they might be seeing but haven't articulated. If every "yes" profile has results-oriented language and every "no" profile describes responsibilities without outcomes, you can surface that: "It seems like the way people describe their achievements matters to you — the people you'd reach out to tend to quantify impact, and the ones you'd skip describe their role in more general terms." You're connecting the bank to their specific reactions, not teaching them a framework.

**Don't present the categories.** This is an organizing structure for Claude, not for the user. Never surface these as categories, lists, or menus. If the subject says "I need to know if this person actually has authority," you might draw from the scope and ownership section to ask sharper questions — but you'd never say "let's look at the scope and ownership signals" or "here are five things to consider."

**Every company's version is different.** A signal that's critical for one company is irrelevant for another. The user's profile reviews and reactions tell you which of these categories carry the most weight. Lead with what they're already noticing, then broaden from there.

**Signals are hypotheses until tested.** A signal from this bank that the user hasn't validated against real profiles is a suggestion, not a recommendation. Frame it that way: "This might be worth looking at" not "you should track this."
